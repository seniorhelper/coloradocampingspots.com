BLM Camping in Colorado

The Bureau of Land Management oversees millions of acres in Colorado, most of it west of the Continental Divide. Its canyons, mesas and sagebrush valleys offer free and low-cost camping, with a Colorado-specific stay rule that differs from what many travelers expect.

National forests get most of the attention in Colorado camping, but the Bureau of Land Management's lands on the Western Slope and in southern Colorado deliver a different kind of trip: red canyons, juniper-dotted mesas, river corridors and wide skies with few people. Camping on BLM land ranges from free, primitive spots off a dirt road to developed campgrounds with toilets and tables. The rules are simple, but one of them catches out-of-state visitors.

The Colorado BLM occupancy rule

BLM Colorado has its own supplementary rules on camping and occupancy, published in the Federal Register in 2010 and repeated in a 2025 BLM fact sheet. In plain language:

  • 14 daysYou may camp at one location for up to 14 days within any 30-day period.
  • 30 air milesOnce you reach 14 days, you must move at least 30 air miles from that location.
  • 30 days awayYou may not return to the original location for 30 days.
  • Applies widelyThe rule covers camping at any one location on BLM-managed public land, including campgrounds, unless a site posts different limits.

Not the 25-mile rule

Many BLM guides online describe a 14-day stay, a 25-mile move and a 28-day absence. That pattern comes from other states' guidance. In Colorado, plan around 14 days in 30, 30 air miles and 30 days away, and confirm with the local field office.

Where BLM land is in Colorado

BLM-managed land is concentrated in the western half of the state and in a few southern basins. Broadly, you will find it:

  • Around Grand Junction and Fruita, including the McInnis Canyons and Dominguez-Escalante national conservation areas.
  • Around Montrose, Delta and Gunnison, including canyon country near the Gunnison River.
  • In the Four Corners region near Cortez and Dolores, including Canyons of the Ancients National Monument, where cultural sites are protected by federal law.
  • Across the San Luis Valley and in pockets of the upper Arkansas River valley.
  • In northwest Colorado around Craig, Meeker and Kremmling.

Land ownership is a patchwork, with private land, state trust land and BLM land side by side. Use a current land-ownership map or app and respect private property and posted closures. State trust lands generally are not open to camping.

Developed BLM campgrounds

Developed BLM campgrounds usually offer vault toilets, picnic tables and fire rings, sometimes shade structures, and occasionally water. Some charge a nightly fee and some are reservable. Near Grand Junction, for example, the Rabbit Valley sites moved to Recreation.gov, with roughly half of the sites on a six-month window and the rest on a four-day window. Sites like the North Fruita Desert area are popular bases for mountain bikers. Our best camping spots page covers several Grand Valley options.

Desert camping is different

Many BLM camps sit in arid country where the hazards differ from the mountains:

  1. Carry water. Plan on carrying all of it; natural sources are scarce, silty or seasonal.
  2. Respect flash floods. Never camp in a dry wash or narrow canyon bottom. A storm miles away can send water through without warning.
  3. Watch the roads. Clay roads can become impassable when wet, and sandy tracks can trap two-wheel-drive vehicles. Check recent weather and carry recovery gear.
  4. Time the heat. Summer afternoons in low desert valleys are hot. Spring and fall are the prime seasons for canyon country.
  5. Stay on durable surfaces. Biological soil crust, the dark knobby layer on desert soil, takes decades to recover once crushed. Park and walk on existing roads, rock and gravel.

Fire on BLM land

Each BLM district in Colorado issues its own fire restriction orders, often in coordination with neighboring national forests and counties. Under Stage 1, campfires are generally limited to designated, permanently installed metal grates in developed recreation sites, and fires of any type are prohibited outside developed areas. Stage 2 adds wider bans, in some districts including charcoal grills and wood stoves in developed sites. Fireworks are prohibited at all times on national forest land and are generally not allowed on BLM land either. Check the BLM Colorado fire restrictions page and the local field office before each trip; see our fire restrictions guide for a full explanation.

Cultural sites and artifacts

Southwest Colorado has an exceptional density of archaeological sites, including Ancestral Puebloan structures, rock art and artifacts. Federal law protects them. Do not camp in or next to ruins, do not touch rock art, and leave artifacts where you find them. Photograph them and move on.

Good habits on BLM land

  • Camp on previously used spots and keep vehicles on existing roads.
  • Pack out all trash, and pack out or properly dispose of human waste.
  • Keep dogs under control around livestock and wildlife; grazing is common on BLM land.
  • Leave gates as you found them.
  • Expect little or no cell service and tell someone your plans.

Frequently asked questions

What is the BLM camping limit in Colorado?

BLM Colorado's supplementary rules limit camping to 14 days within any 30-day period at one location. After reaching 14 days, campers must move at least 30 air miles away and may not return to the original location for 30 days. Many people expect the 14-day, 25-mile, 28-day pattern used in some other states, but Colorado's rule is stricter.

Is BLM camping free in Colorado?

Dispersed camping on most BLM land is free. Developed BLM campgrounds may charge a fee, and some, such as the Rabbit Valley sites near Grand Junction, take reservations on Recreation.gov.

Can you have a campfire on BLM land in Colorado?

Only when no restrictions prohibit it and local rules allow it. BLM field offices issue their own fire restriction orders, and during Stage 1 restrictions, fires are generally limited to permanent metal grates in developed recreation sites. Check the BLM Colorado fire restrictions page before every trip.

Where can I find BLM land in Colorado?

BLM land is concentrated around Grand Junction, Montrose, Gunnison, Durango, Cortez, the San Luis Valley and northwest Colorado. BLM field office pages, the agency's maps and land-ownership layers in many mapping apps show where public land begins and ends.
